Formulas

  • Frequency offset at time t:  Δf(t) = Δf₀ + a·t_day  (fractional, ppb)
  • Accumulated time error:  TE(t) = Δf₀·t_s + a·t_s² / (2·86400)  (seconds)
  •    where t_s = t in seconds, Δf₀ in fractional units (1 ppb = 10⁻⁹), a in fractional units per day
  • Time to threshold TEth:  t = ( −Δf₀ + √(Δf₀² + 2·a·TEth·/86400) ) / (a/86400)  for a > 0

The linear term is the initial frequency error integrated over time; the quadratic term is the accumulated aging. The model assumes constant temperature and linear aging — real oscillators also carry a temperature coefficient (e.g. 5×10⁻⁹ per °C for a mid-grade OCXO, which can dominate an uncontrolled shelter) and aging is not perfectly linear after retrace. TE sign: positive means the local clock is running fast relative to true time.

What is holdover?

A GNSS-disciplined clock continuously steers a local oscillator to GNSS time. When the GNSS signal disappears — antenna failure, jamming, spoofing detection shutdown, indoor or urban-canyon outage — the oscillator "holds over": it free-runs on its own stability, and time error accumulates as the product of frequency error and elapsed time. Holdover specifications (e.g. "< 1 μs per 24 h" or "< 10 μs per day") are among the most important figures for telecom boundary clocks, cellular TDD networks, phasor measurement units and defense platforms.

Why GNSS-denied timing matters: jamming & spoofing

  • Jamming raises the noise floor so receivers cannot track satellites. Regions with chronic GPS L1 interference are well documented; a jammed site falls into holdover without warning unless monitored.
  • Spoofing is worse: forged signals look valid and can silently shift a network's time. Robust receivers detect anomalies and deliberately enter holdover — turning oscillator quality into the last line of defense.
  • Holdover budget drives hardware cost. The required holdover duration (minutes for urban handset use, 24 h+ for telecom nodes, days for isolated defense sites) selects the oscillator class: TCXO → OCXO → rubidium → caesium/maser. This calculator quantifies that trade.

How to extend holdover capability

  1. Start clean. Pre-align the frequency before GNSS loss: a well-disciplined OCXO entering holdover with |Δf₀| < 0.1 ppb buys 10× more time than one at 1 ppb. Long averaging time constants before the outage pay off here.
  2. Control temperature. Aging presets assume a stable oven. A shelter that swings 10 °C can add tens of ppb through the OCXO temperature coefficient — often dominating aging. Insulate, heat, or measure and compensate.
  3. Dual-source architectures. Pair a rubidium or high-grade OCXO with a second reference (e.g. a cesium standard or eLoran/other PNT source) and cross-check during holdover; switch or correct when sources disagree beyond a threshold.
  4. Know your aging curve. Aging improves after burn-in (weeks–months). Use the device's datasheet daily-aging figure for steady state, not the initial turn-on drift.
